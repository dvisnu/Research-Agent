# Literature-Grounded Novelty Assessment — Extraction Notes

> **Paper:** *Literature-Grounded Novelty Assessment of Scientific Ideas*
> **Authors:** Simra Shahid (Microsoft), Marissa Radensky, Raymond Fok (Univ. of Washington), Pao Siangliulue, Daniel S. Weld, Tom Hope (Allen Institute for AI)
> **arXiv:** 2506.22026v1 [cs.IR], 27 Jun 2025
> **Code/data:** https://github.com/simra-shahid/idea_novelty_checker
> **System name:** **Idea Novelty Checker**
> **Related:** same group as **Scideator** (Radensky et al.) — the ideas evaluated here were largely generated by Scideator. Read the two together.

---

## 1. TL;DR

**This is the most directly relevant paper in the whole set for Component 4 of our architecture.** It is entirely about the problem our docx flags as the central risk: *"if the agent searches 50 papers and finds no prior work, that does not by itself mean the idea is novel — the search may simply have failed."*

Their answer: novelty assessment is **a retrieval problem first and a judgement problem second**. The system is a **retrieve-then-rerank RAG pipeline**:

1. **Broad candidate retrieval** — three parallel channels: LLM-generated keyword/title queries, **whole-idea snippet search**, and seed-paper recommendations.
2. **Two-stage reranking** — SPECTER-2 embedding filter (→ top N=100), then **facet-based RankGPT** reranking (→ top k=10).
3. **Novelty judgement** — LLM classifies novel/not-novel with **literature-grounded reasoning**, steered by **15 expert-labeled in-context examples**.

The headline empirical claim: **~13% higher expert agreement than AI Researcher (Si et al.), and >10× the agreement of AI Scientist (Lu et al.).** The ablation is the striking part — dropping facet-based reranking alone collapses "not novel" detection accuracy from **89.66% → 13.79%**.

## 2. The Formative Study (why the design looks like this)

Before building anything, they ran an expert annotation study — worth reading as a cautionary tale about our own evaluation plan.

**Round 1.** Two expert annotators (the first two authors) judged **51 ideas** (46 from Scideator, 5 adapted from accepted/rejected OpenReview papers at ICLR'22 / NeurIPS'23) into **three** categories: *novel / moderately novel / not novel*. Relevant papers were found by keyword retrieval + LLM reranking.

Result: **Cohen's κ = 0.64** (moderate). Two failure causes identified:
- Experts fell back on **broader domain knowledge** instead of restricting judgement to the retrieved papers — because the top papers alone were often *insufficient*.
- **Three categories caused disagreement** — the novel/moderately-novel boundary is itself subjective.

**Round 2.** Two changes: (a) judge **solely** on the provided papers, (b) collapse to **binary** novel/not-novel. Reannotated 51 ideas (34 new from Radensky et al. + 17 from the round where external knowledge had leaked in).

Result: **Cohen's κ = 0.68**, fewer disagreements. Of the 8 remaining disagreements: 4 = one expert overlooked a paper detail, 2 = differing perception of subtle contributions, 2 = no comment given.

**Two challenges this crystallised** — these drive the whole architecture:
- **C1: high-quality retrieval** of the papers relevant to the idea. (There are hundreds of millions of potential matches.)
- **C2: a clear, fixed criterion** for what counts as novel, to counteract subjectivity.

> **Direct lesson for us:** even *human experts* only reach κ≈0.68 on binary novelty when constrained to a fixed paper set — and κ≈0.64 with three categories. **Our 5-level novelty ladder will have worse inter-annotator agreement than that.** We should expect it, measure it, and consider whether the levels are evidence-defined (which ours are — level 4 requires an explicit literature-stated limitation, level 5 requires a consistent gap across multiple papers) rather than intuition-defined. Evidence-anchored levels are defensible in a way "moderately novel" is not.

## 3. Their definition of novelty (the C2 answer) — facets

Building on a line of HCI/ideation work (Portenoy, Kang, Chan & Chang, Suh, Srinivasan & Chan, Choi, Radensky), they decompose an idea into **core facets**:

| Facet | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | The problem being addressed / the objective. |
| **Mechanism** | The proposed technical solution/approach. |
| **Evaluation** | The validation method. |
| *(Application domain)* | *Used as a fourth axis in reranking.* |

**The novelty rule, verbatim in substance:**

> An idea is **novel** if it differs from **all** retrieved papers in **at least one core facet** — purpose (a distinct objective), mechanism (a distinct technical approach), or evaluation (a distinct validation method). An idea is **also** novel if it **uniquely combines** these facets, or **applies them to a new application domain**.

This is a *conjunctive-over-papers, disjunctive-over-facets* rule, and it is operationally checkable — which is exactly what a subjective judgement needs.

## 4. Architecture

```
                        INPUT IDEA  (+ optional seed papers)
                                  │
┌─────────────── STEP 1: CANDIDATE RETRIEVAL (three channels) ───────────────┐
│  (a) LLM_query extracts keywords + candidate titles → Semantic Scholar     │
│      Search API                                                            │
│  (b) Whole idea → Semantic Scholar **Snippet search** (~500-word snippets) │
│  (c) Seed papers → Semantic Scholar **Recommendations API** (related work) │
└────────────────────────────── union → candidate pool ─────────────────────┘
                                  │
┌────────────── STEP 2: TWO-STAGE RERANKING ────────────────────────────────┐
│  (a) SPECTER-2 embeddings, cosine similarity vs idea  →  top **N = 100**  │
│  (b) **Facet-based RankGPT** (LLM_rankgpt)             →  top **k = 10**  │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                  │
┌────────────── STEP 3: NOVELTY EVALUATION ─────────────────────────────────┐
│  LLM_novelty( idea, top-k papers (title+abstract),                        │
│               15 expert-labeled in-context examples )                     │
│      → binary class {novel, not novel} + literature-grounded reasoning    │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Step 1 — Candidate retrieval (three complementary channels)

The point of three channels is coverage: *"simple retrieval methods often overlook important aspects of an idea."*

- **Query-based / keyword retrieval.** `LLM_query` generates search queries from **keywords *and potential titles*** related to the idea; each query hits the **Semantic Scholar Search API**; results are unioned. (Generating plausible *titles* as queries is a nice trick — it moves the query into the same register as the target documents.)
- **Snippet retrieval.** Semantic Scholar's **Snippet Search API**, trained to find similar ~500-word passages. Because the retrieval context is large, **they feed the entire idea in as the query** rather than keywords. This is the channel that catches papers with no keyword overlap.
- **Seed-paper expansion.** If the idea came with source papers, use the **Semantic Scholar Recommendations API** to pull their related work.

### Step 2 — Two-stage reranking

**Stage A — embedding filter.** SPECTER-2 (Cohan et al., citation-informed document embeddings) → cosine similarity → **top N = 100**. Fast but *"limited in its capacity to capture deeper and more contextual relationships between different facets."*

**Stage B — facet-based RankGPT.** They take RankGPT (Sun et al.) and **replace its generic relevance criterion with an explicit facet-priority ordering**. The stated priority ladder:

1. papers matching **all** key facets of the idea;
2. then papers aligning with **application domain + purpose**;
3. then papers sharing similarity in **purpose, mechanism, or evaluation**;
4. lowest: papers that only partially match or address merely related facets.

**This substitution — generic relevance → facet-priority relevance — is the single highest-value idea in the paper**, and the ablation proves it (§7).

### Step 3 — Novelty evaluation

`LLM_novelty` receives the idea + the top-k papers (**titles and abstracts**) + `n_examples` in-context examples drawn from the formative study. Output: binary class + reasoning grounded in the retrieved literature.

Example output from the paper:

```
Classification: Not Novel
Reasoning: The idea is not novel because it closely resembles existing tools like
'Bursting Scientific Filter Bubbles'[4], Bridger[9] and ResearchRabbit[3], which also
use citation-based suggestions and visualizations. The proposed tool's combination of
citation networks and author profiles is already well-covered by these systems.
```

Note the **inline bracketed indices pointing at specific retrieved papers** — a lightweight form of provenance, and the format our Novel Evidence Report's "Closest Existing Work" field should produce.

## 5. Implementation Details

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Embedding model | **SPECTER-2** (Cohan et al.) |
| Embedding filter output | **N = 100** papers |
| Final paper set | **k = 10** papers |
| `LLM_query`, `LLM_rankgpt`, `LLM_novelty` | **gpt-4o** (used Aug–Sep 2024) |
| Ablation config | `o3-mini` for novelty (Step 3), `gpt-4o` for reranking (Step 2) |
| In-context examples (best) | **15 idea examples**, random seed 100 |
| OpenReview-example baseline (best) | 5 idea-review pairs |
| DSPy config | 2 bootstrapped examples, 12 prompt iterations |
| TextGRAD config | 12 prompt iterations |
| Retrieval APIs | Semantic Scholar **Search**, **Snippet Text**, **Recommendations** |
| Reranker | **RankGPT** (Sun et al.), facet-modified |

**Dataset:** 67 consensus-labeled examples from the formative study (**39 novel, 28 non-novel**), split **35 train / 32 test**, balanced.

**Ablation dataset:** 58 ideas = 13 'not novel' from the test set + 45 NLP papers from the literature. They deliberately evaluate on **'not novel' cases only**, with a good justification: *"the ideas labeled novel in expert-labeled test data can vary with different retrieved paper sets."* (i.e. a "novel" label is only valid relative to a fixed corpus, so it can't be used to compare retrieval variants — only "not novel" is stable, because the matching paper exists.) **This is a subtle and important experimental-design point we should copy.**

> **Note on prompts:** the paper does **not** include a prompt appendix — it states *"All prompts are provided in the anonymised codebase."* What is recoverable is the facet-priority ladder (§4 Stage B), the novelty definition (§3), and the in-context example format (§8).

## 6. Baselines

| Baseline | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Zero-shot** | Straightforward manually-written prompt. |
| **+ Anthropic prompt generator** | The same prompt refined via Anthropic's prompt-generator tool. |
| **DSPy** (Khattab et al.) | Prompt optimisation, with and without reasoning fields. |
| **TextGRAD** (Yuksekgonul et al.) | "Textual differentiation" prompt optimisation. |
| **OpenReview examples** | In-context examples built from real ICLR/NeurIPS reviews instead of their own expert labels. ~8,156 submissions filtered for novelty discussion; reviews manually selected that evaluate *the core idea* rather than the whole paper; 20 idea-review pairs sampled. Input title+abstract style-adapted to match the idea format. |
| **AI Scientist** (Lu et al.) | Its *novelty checker* prompt (not its paper reviewer). |
| **AI Researcher** (Si et al.) | Swiss-system tournament comparing ideas pairwise against individual papers; not novel if any comparison is sufficiently similar. |

**Fair-comparison control:** for AI Scientist and AI Researcher they compare **prompts only**, on the same test set and a **fixed top-10 paper set**, because different retrieval would change the classification and confound the comparison.

### Their critique of AI Scientist (worth internalising — we will be tempted by the same shortcuts)
1. Depends on **keyword-based retrieval**, which fails when the relevant paper doesn't contain the exact keyword.
2. Compares an idea against a **large number of retrieved papers (sometimes >100)**, triggering the known problem that LLMs overlook instructions buried in long prompts.
3. The decision relies on **string matching** for phrases like `"decision made: novel"` / `"decision made: not novel"` — and **if no such decision is reached, the idea is automatically considered novel**. (Independent evaluation by Beel et al. found it misclassifying well-established concepts — e.g. micro-batching for SGD — as novel.)

> **Failing open toward "novel" is the exact failure mode our docx warns about**, implemented as a literal code path in a widely-used system. Our design should fail *closed*: no conclusion → lower the novelty level and say the search was inconclusive.

## 7. Results

### Main comparison (Table 1, gpt-4o, expert-annotated test set)

| Method | Acc | P | R | F1 | κ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-shot | 0.68 | 0.76 | 0.64 | 0.65 | – |
| + Anthropic prompt generator | 0.68 | 0.70 | 0.64 | 0.64 | – |
| DSPy (idea, papers, class) | 0.68 | 0.83 | 0.62 | 0.58 | – |
| DSPy (+ reasoning) | 0.66 | 0.82 | 0.58 | 0.52 | – |
| TextGRAD (idea, papers, class) | 0.78 | 0.76 | 0.76 | 0.76 | – |
| OpenReview examples (idea + review) | 0.59 | 0.55 | 0.51 | 0.43 | – |
| Expert examples (idea, reasoning) | 0.75 | 0.76 | 0.77 | 0.75 | – |
| Expert examples (idea, papers, class) | 0.78 | 0.77 | 0.76 | 0.77 | – |
| **Expert examples (idea, papers, class, reasoning)** | **0.81** | **0.84** | **0.78** | **0.79** | **0.59** |
| AI Scientist (Lu et al.) | 0.47 | 0.55 | 0.53 | 0.44 | **0.05** |
| AI Researcher — GPT-4o | 0.78 | 0.81 | 0.74 | 0.75 | 0.52 |
| AI Researcher — Claude-3.5-Sonnet | 0.56 | 0.63 | 0.61 | 0.56 | 0.19 |

Readings:
- **Expert-labeled in-context examples are the biggest lever.** Full config (idea + papers + class + **reasoning**) beats every prompt-optimiser.
- **Reasoning in the examples matters** (+0.03 acc, +0.02 F1 over class-only) — showing the model *how experts justified* the call, not just what they called it.
- **OpenReview reviews are a bad substitute for expert labels** (0.59 acc) — real peer reviews don't reference the compared papers, so they can't teach grounded reasoning. Even with the papers stripped from the expert examples, expert-labeled still beat OpenReview.
- **Prompt optimisers underperformed** hand-written expert-example prompts. Authors' explanation: too few train/validation examples for DSPy/TextGRAD with gpt-4o. TextGRAD never improved on its own initial system prompt.
- **AI Scientist's κ = 0.05 is essentially chance agreement.** Caveat the authors give honestly: it defaults to "not novel" when it can't conclude — **18 of 32 times** — which distorts its rates.
- **Model choice matters enormously for the same prompt**: AI Researcher scores 0.78 with GPT-4o and 0.56 with Claude-3.5-Sonnet.

### Ablation — accuracy at predicting "not novel" (Table 2)

| Method | Accuracy |
|---|---|
| **Complete System** | **89.66%** |
| – Relevance RankGPT *(facet reranker → generic relevance reranker)* | 13.79% |
| – Embedding Filtering *(no LLM reranker at all)* | 10.34% |
| – Snippet Retrieval *(snippet retrieval only, no rerank)* | 8.62% |
| – Keyword Retrieval *(keyword retrieval only, no rerank)* | 5.17% |

**This is a startling ablation.** Swapping only the reranking *criterion* — generic relevance instead of facet-based — drops accuracy from 89.7% to 13.8%. Raw retrieval without any reranking is near-useless (5–9%).

### Retrieval overlap analysis (Table 3) — why the ablation is so violent

| Method | Overlap with complete-system top-10 (↑) | Rank shift (↓) |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance RankGPT | 7.97 | 0.67 |
| Embedding Filtering | 7.93 | 0.84 |
| Snippet Retrieval | 2.88 | 1.85 |
| Keyword Retrieval | 1.17 | 1.39 |

The subtlety: relevance-RankGPT and embedding-only **still retrieve ~8 of the same 10 papers** — yet accuracy collapses by 76 points. **So the ~2–3 papers that differ, and their ordering, are decisive.** Novelty assessment is exquisitely sensitive to whether the *one* genuinely overlapping paper made the cut. Meanwhile raw snippet/keyword retrieval overlaps by only 1–3 papers, confirming the reranker stages are doing nearly all the work.

> **This is the most important single finding for our C4 design:** getting 8/10 of the right papers is *not* good enough. Novelty verdicts hinge on the tail of the ranking. It is a strong argument for our project to invest in retrieval quality and to report a **retrieval-coverage/confidence signal** alongside any novelty claim — rather than presenting a verdict as if the corpus were complete.

### Prompt sensitivity (§6.4)

TextGRAD optimisation over 12 prompt variants (train=25, val=10, test=32) revealed:
- **None of the 12 optimised prompts beat the original** (final test accuracy 0.78125).
- Prompts with **near-identical instructions** produced wildly different accuracy — e.g. prompt 3 (acc = **0**) and prompt 9 (acc = **0.6**) *both* instructed the model to evaluate whether the idea introduces unique methodologies and how it compares to existing work.
- Interesting behaviours surfaced during optimisation: considering **historical context**, **frequency of similar studies**, **comparative analysis**, and **arguing both the novel and non-novel side**.
- Authors' conclusion: *"Even minor variations in wording and structure can lead to substantial performance changes."* And candidly: *"It remains unclear why certain prompts perform better despite having similar instructions."*

### Qualitative

- **Idea Novelty Checker** gives concise justifications naming specific similarities/differences with existing work.
- **AI Researcher** evaluates each paper individually and calls an idea not-novel if any paper is "citable"; in their examples **no paper was flagged citable despite sharing similar purposes**, yielding a false "novel."
- **AI Scientist** sometimes **misinterprets the idea itself** — in one case shifting its focus from the idea to the accompanying code.

## 8. In-context example format (reconstructable from Table 4)

Each expert-labeled example is a triple: **Idea → Most Relevant Papers (titles) → Reasoning**. Two real examples:

**Example 1 — labeled NOVEL**
```
Idea: Develop a natural language processing classifier designed to improve scientific
paper revisions by automatically identifying and categorizing reviewer comments that are
most likely to lead to substantial and actionable revisions. [...]

Most Relevant Papers:
 1. ARIES: A Corpus of Scientific Paper Edits Made in Response to Peer Reviews
 2. Can large language models provide useful feedback on research papers?
 3. A Dataset of Peer Reviews (PeerRead)
 ...
10. ReviVal: Towards Automatically Evaluating the Informativeness of Peer Reviews

Reasoning: The idea is novel because it uniquely focuses on prioritizing reviewer
comments for actionable revisions, which is not explicitly addressed in ARIES[1] or
other works like ReviVal[10].
```

**Example 2 — labeled NOT NOVEL**
```
Idea: Develop a systematic review-based framework designed to align LLM evaluation with
human preferences, ensuring that evaluation criteria are continuously refined based on
comprehensive reviews of user feedback and emerging model behaviors. [...]

Most Relevant Papers:
 1. EvalLM: Interactive Evaluation of Large Language Model Prompts on User-Defined Criteria
 2. Humanely: Human evaluation of LLM yield, using a novel web-based evaluation tool
 ...
10. Prometheus 2: An Open Source Language Model Specialized in Evaluating Other Language Models

Reasoning: The idea is not novel because it closely resembles existing frameworks like
EvalLM[1] and HumanELY[2], which already align LLM evaluations with human preferences
using user-defined criteria and human feedback.
```

Note the reasoning style in both: **name the specific closest paper(s) by index, and state the precise facet on which the idea does or does not differ.** That's the target output format for our "Difference from Existing Work" field.

## 9. What to take into our Research Agent

| Our component | What this paper gives us |
|---|---|
| **C2 — Literature Acquisition** | **Three retrieval channels, not one.** Keyword/title-query generation + **whole-idea snippet search** + seed-paper recommendations. The snippet channel is the one that catches semantically-matching papers with no keyword overlap — precisely the failure our docx names. All three are free Semantic Scholar APIs (Search / Snippet Text / Recommendations). |
| **C3 — Research Knowledge Layer** | The **facet decomposition (purpose / mechanism / evaluation / application domain)** is a better organising schema for novelty comparison than our current flat `methodology / datasets / experiments` fields. It composes cleanly with our graph — store facets per paper, and gap detection becomes "which facet combination is unoccupied." |
| **C4 — Gap / Novelty Detection** | The core of the paper. Adopt: (a) the **operational novelty rule** (differs in ≥1 facet from *all* retrieved papers, or uniquely combines facets, or new application domain); (b) **facet-priority reranking** as the retrieval-quality mechanism; (c) **expert-labeled in-context examples *with reasoning***, which beat every prompt optimiser; (d) the output format — classification + reasoning that **names the closest papers by index and the differing facet**. Their pipeline maps directly onto our *Closest Existing Work* and *Difference from Existing Work* report fields. |
| **C4 — the 5-level ladder specifically** | Their limitation section states the future direction: *"refining our novelty evaluation to view novelty as a continuum rather than binary classification."* **Our 5-level potential-novelty scale is literally their stated future work.** But their formative study is the warning: three categories dropped human κ from 0.68 to 0.64. If we keep five levels, the levels must be **evidence-defined** (level 4 = literature explicitly states the limitation; level 5 = consistent gap across multiple papers) rather than intuition-defined, and we must report inter-annotator agreement. |
| **C5 — Experiment Planning** | Less direct, but the facet framing transfers: an experiment plan's *evaluation* facet is exactly what our Baselines/Metrics fields encode, and "differs in evaluation method" is a legitimate novelty axis our plan currently under-uses. |
| **C-Eval — Evaluation** | (a) **Cohen's κ against expert labels** is the right headline metric, and κ≈0.59 for a good system / κ≈0.68 for humans sets realistic expectations. (b) **Ablate on "not novel" cases only** — a "novel" label isn't stable across retrieval variants, so it can't be used to compare retrieval. (c) **Fix the paper set when comparing prompts**, vary it only when comparing retrieval. (d) **Report retrieval overlap and rank shift** (Table 3 style) — it explains *why* an ablation moved. (e) Expect and measure **prompt sensitivity**; do not assume an optimiser will help. |

**Cheap wins to steal first:** (1) snippet search on the whole idea as a retrieval channel; (2) facet-priority RankGPT reranking; (3) the facet-based novelty rule as our operational definition; (4) in-context examples that include *expert reasoning*, not just labels; (5) **fail closed** — never default to "novel" when the system can't conclude.

## 10. Gaps, Limitations & Future Scope

### Stated by the authors (§8)
1. **Only top-10 papers are considered**, forced by context limits (15 in-context examples eat the budget). Those 10 *"may disproportionately influence the overall novelty assessment."*
2. **Annotator circularity.** The same two annotators who produced the in-context examples also labeled the test ideas — which *"could potentially give our approach an advantage in understanding our view of novelty."* An unusually honest admission, and it means the +13% over AI Researcher is probably an upper bound.
3. **Idea-source circularity.** Most test ideas were generated by **Scideator**, the same system that produced the in-context examples (some came from OpenReview).
4. **Binary classification is too coarse** — their stated future work is to treat novelty as a **continuum**.
5. **Stated future direction:** expand literature scope using **OpenAI Deep Research** and **ScholarQA / Ai2 Scholar QA** (Singh et al.).
6. **Unexplained prompt sensitivity** — near-identical instructions give 0 vs 0.6 accuracy, and they cannot say why.

### Gaps we can attack (our project's opening)
1. **No retrieval-confidence signal.** The system's entire weakness — and it admits this via the top-10 limitation — is that a "novel" verdict is only as good as the corpus searched, and **it never reports how well it searched**. Their own Table 3 proves the verdict flips on 2–3 papers. **A calibrated coverage/confidence estimate attached to every novelty claim is unclaimed space, and it is the whole justification for our "potential novelty, never proven novelty" framing.**
2. **Title + abstract only.** Novelty is judged from titles and abstracts of 10 papers. A paper whose *method section* contains the overlapping idea but whose abstract doesn't mention it will be missed entirely. Our C2 schema reads deeper.
3. **No provenance beyond bracketed indices.** The reasoning cites `[1]`, `[4]` — better than nothing, but there is no span-level grounding, no claim→section link, and nothing to verify the cited paper actually supports the stated similarity. **Citation correctness and provenance consistency (our §4.2 metrics) are entirely unmeasured here.**
4. **Judges the idea, doesn't generate it.** This is a *checker*, not an ideator — which is good news: it slots into our C4 as a component rather than competing with our system. Its natural pairing is CoI's generation loop (whose own novelty check, `Similar: 0/1` over a handful of papers, is far weaker than this pipeline).
5. **No gap reasoning.** It tells you an idea overlaps with existing work; it never tells you **what is missing** or **what would make it novel**. Contrast GAPMAP, which infers gaps but never checks whether they're closed. **Nobody has combined the two — gap inference + literature-grounded novelty verification is exactly our C4, and it is open.**
6. **Tiny dataset.** 67 labeled examples, 32 in test, 58 in ablation, 2 annotators. Statistically thin for the strength of the claims — and the authors' κ dropped to 0.59 for the system vs 0.68 human-human.
7. **Expensive per assessment.** LLM query generation + snippet search + 100-paper embedding + RankGPT over 100 + a 15-example novelty prompt, per idea, on gpt-4o. No cost or latency numbers are given anywhere in the paper.
8. **Domain-bound to CS/NLP.** All ideas, all expert annotators, all the ablation papers are NLP/HCI. The facet decomposition is plausibly general but is never tested outside CS.
9. **Static, one-shot.** No iteration: a "not novel" verdict doesn't trigger idea refinement, and no critique loop turns the closest-existing-work into a *differentiation* suggestion. Combining this checker with CoI's `[Bad case]` regeneration loop would close that circuit.
